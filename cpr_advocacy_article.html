Stayin’ Alive: Why the
Next Hero Needs to be You

A CAS Advocacy Project | Health & Service

Cardiac arrest doesn't wait for a doctor to arrive. It happens in gyms, cafeterias, and living rooms. In those first four minutes, the difference between a tragedy and a miracle isn't a medical degree—it's a bystander with two hands and a bit of courage.

Most people hesitate to act during an emergency because they are afraid of "doing it wrong." But here is the medical reality: the only "wrong" thing to do in a cardiac emergency is to do nothing at all.

Breaking the Myths

You might worry about legal trouble or accidentally hurting someone. However, Good Samaritan Laws are designed specifically to protect people like you who step in to help. While chest compressions need to be forceful—and yes, you might feel a rib crack—that is a small price to pay for a life saved.

The 4-Minute Window: Brain damage begins just four minutes after the heart stops. By the time an ambulance arrives, it might be too late. Bystander CPR can double or triple a victim's chance of survival.

Hands-Only: Keep it Simple

Modern guidelines have moved away from mouth-to-mouth for bystanders. "Hands-Only" CPR is just as effective in the first few minutes. If you see someone collapse:

  • 1. CHECK: Ensure the area is safe and tap their shoulder to see if they respond.
  • 2. CALL: Point to someone and say "Call 911 and get an AED." Be specific.
  • 3. COMPRESS: Push hard and fast in the center of the chest.

The Rhythm of Life

To keep blood flowing to the brain, you need to push at a rate of 100 to 120 beats per minute. That sounds like a lot of pressure, but you probably already know the tempo by heart.

Look Around You

Our school is equipped with AEDs (Automated External Defibrillators). These boxes are not just for nurses. They talk to you, giving step-by-step vocal instructions. They will never shock someone who doesn't need it. Today, take 30 seconds to find the AED nearest to your locker. It’s a small bit of homework that could save a life.
